\label{sec:reranking}

\paragraph{Reranker fine-tuning beats decision fine-tuning.}
On every backbone from 0.6B to 9B, the reranker fine-tune ranks better than the decision fine-tune in either mode
(Table~\ref{tab:triplets}). Averaged over the ten collections, its margin over the better decision mode ranges from
5.2 points nDCG@10 at Qwen3.5-9B (Prism-9B 59.0 vs.\ Clef-Flash 53.8) to 28.8 points at Qwen3-0.6B (56.1 vs.\ 27.3).
The difference is significant on 48 of the 60 pairs of reranker fine-tune and collection, and the decision fine-tune
is never significantly better (${}^\ast$). The margin does not come from training on MS~MARCO: on the zero-shot
subset, which no fine-tune was trained on, it ranges from 5.4 to 23.9 points. Nor does it come from the undisclosed
recipes of the published rerankers: Ours-9B, trained on MS~MARCO alone with standard losses, matches Prism-9B (59.4
vs.\ 59.0 on average, 56.1 vs.\ 53.7 on the zero-shot subset). At 27B, the two kinds of fine-tuning tie. Our reranker
fine-tune of the checkpoint that Kev-27B starts from reaches 59.6 against Kev-27B's 60.1, and 54.9 against 55.0 on
the zero-shot subset; they differ significantly on one collection only, DBPedia, where ours is better. On no backbone
does decision fine-tuning make the better reranker.

\paragraph{Up to 9B, decision fine-tuning adds nothing over the base model.}
Up to 9B, the better mode of a decision fine-tune stays within $-2.5$ and $+0.7$ points of its zero-shot base model,
asked the same question, with significant gains on at most three of ten collections (${}^\dagger$). Reranker
fine-tuning of the same backbones adds 5.9 (Prism-9B) to 29.5 points (Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B). A user who takes the
released Qwen3.5-4B or Qwen3.5-9B and asks it whether a passage answers the query gets the same ranking as from the
decision fine-tunes built on it, at no training cost. At 27B, both kinds of fine-tuning add about the same to the base
model (56.4): Kev-27B 3.7 points, significantly on eight collections, and our reranker fine-tune 3.2. Clef, the other
27B decision fine-tune, adds nothing (54.8 joint, 53.9 isolated).

\paragraph{Context.}
Table~\ref{tab:context} places the triplets among other rerankers on the same candidates. \jev{} (59.1) ranks as well
as the reranker fine-tunes of 8B and 9B (59.0 to 59.4) and the Set-Encoder (59.0). The open Kev-27B (60.1) matches
the closed model it imitates, and so does our 27B reranker fine-tune (59.6): there is no secret in \jev{} that a
fine-tune of a strong open backbone cannot reach, with or without decision data. \laya{} (43.5) does not improve on
its BM25 candidates (43.2).

\paragraph{Joint scoring helps only the largest models.}
Scoring all candidates in one state gains about one point for Kev-27B (60.1 vs.\ 59.0) and Clef (54.8 vs.\ 53.9), but
costs Kev-0.6B 12.2 points and Kev-8B 13.0 (${}^\ddagger$; significant on all ten and on five collections). A likely
reason is the length of the state. A joint state of 100 passages has about 13k tokens on TREC DL and 40k or more on
BEIR, whereas Kev-0.8B and Kev-4B were trained on states of at most 7,552 tokens, and Kev-27B on states of up to
32,768~\cite{KevCards}.
