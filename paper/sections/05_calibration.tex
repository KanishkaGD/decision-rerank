\section{Are Decision Models Better Calibrated?}
\label{sec:calibration}

\input{tables/calibration}

Calibration is the main selling point of decision models. Table~\ref{tab:calibration} gives the ECE, the Brier score
(the mean squared error, which also rewards separating relevant from non-relevant passages) and the count error. With S1Rank's question, decision fine-tuning
improves on the poorly calibrated base models, but no more than reranker fine-tuning: up to 8B, the reranker
fine-tune has a similar or lower ECE and a lower Brier score (e.g.\ Prism-4B 0.218 and 0.172 vs.\ Kev-4B 0.216 and
0.207). At 9B, Clef-Flash beats Prism (0.140 vs.\ 0.242), but Ours-9B is better still (0.111).

\paragraph{The question matters.}
S1Rank's question also accepts passages that only partly address the query; our relevance labels do not. Asking exactly that (\emph{Answers?}) improves every decision and base model, with hardly
any change in ranking. Now the decision fine-tunes beat the published reranker fine-tunes from 4B on (e.g.\ Kev-8B
0.113 vs.\ Qwen3-Reranker-8B 0.257). So their calibration is not robust: it depends on how the question is worded,
as it does for the untuned models (Qwen3-8B: 0.383 to 0.231). Reranker fine-tunes have no question to word; ours
matches Clef-Flash (0.111 vs.\ 0.112).

\paragraph{Uses of calibration.}
Better calibration does not make decision models select better passages. If every passage with $p \ge 0.5$ is kept, the reranker fine-tune keeps the better set on every foundation model, with either question,
because it ranks better. When counting a query's relevant passages with S1Rank's question, no decision model is
clearly better than always guessing the median count (8.4 for Clef vs.\ 8.6). With the stricter question
they are, but the untuned Qwen3.8-27B counts best (5.8), and Ours-9B counts better than Clef-Flash (7.3
vs.\ 8.1).
