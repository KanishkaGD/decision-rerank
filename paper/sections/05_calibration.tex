\section{Are Decision Models Better Calibrated?}
\label{sec:calibration}

\input{tables/calibration}

Calibration is the main selling point of decision models. Besides the ECE we report the Brier score, the mean squared
error of the probabilities, which also rewards separating relevant from non-relevant passages
(Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). With S1Rank's question, decision fine-tuning
improves on the poorly calibrated base models, but no more than reranker fine-tuning: up to 8B, the reranker
fine-tune has a similar or lower ECE and a lower Brier score (e.g.\ Prism-4B 0.218 and 0.172 vs.\ Kev-4B 0.216 and
0.207). At 9B, Clef-Flash beats Prism (0.140 vs.\ 0.242), but Ours-9B is better still (0.111).

\paragraph{The question matters.}
S1Rank's question also accepts passages that only partly address the query, but we count a passage as relevant only
if it answers the query. Asking exactly that (\emph{Answers?}) improves every decision and base model, with hardly
any change in ranking. Now the decision fine-tunes beat the published reranker fine-tunes from 4B on (e.g.\ Kev-8B
0.113 vs.\ Qwen3-Reranker-8B 0.257). But this is not special to decision fine-tuning: Ours-9B matches Clef-Flash
(0.111 vs.\ 0.112), and the untuned models gain too (Qwen3-8B: 0.383 to 0.231).

\paragraph{Uses of calibration.}
Better calibration does not make decision models select better passages. If every passage with $p \ge 0.5$ is kept,
e.g.\ for a generator, the reranker fine-tune keeps the better set on every foundation model, with either question,
because it ranks better. Using the probabilities to count a query's relevant passages works best for the untuned
Qwen3.8-27B (details in our repository).
