\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Cross-encoders are a well-established way to rerank the candidates of a first-stage
retriever~\cite{Nogueira2019,Nogueira2020monoT5}, and open large language models fine-tuned for ranking are the
current state of the art~\cite{Ma2023RankLLaMA,Zhang2025Qwen3Embedding,Zhang2026Prism}. \emph{System One models},
also called decision models, promise something else. They answer a question about an input with a calibrated
probability instead of generated text, in the spirit of fast, intuitive thinking~\cite{Kahneman2011,TypeSafe2026Jev}.
TypeSafe AI released the first of them, \jev{}, on 15 September 2026, trained with an unpublished method called RLCD
(reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions)~\cite{TypeSafe2026Jev}. Within weeks, \jev{} was reported to be a
competitive reranker~\cite{S1Rank,JevRerank} and was evaluated or integrated in retrieval
systems~\cite{Elastic2026,LanceDB2026,Hindsight2026}. Open decision models followed: Kev~\cite{Kev} and Cloudflare's
Clef~\cite{Clef}. They are fine-tuned from Qwen backbones, the same backbones that dedicated rerankers such as
Qwen3-Reranker~\cite{Zhang2025Qwen3Embedding} and Prism~\cite{Zhang2026Prism} are built on.

This makes a clean test possible, and the reports so far have not run it. They compare \jev{} with rerankers built
on other, mostly smaller models, so they cannot tell how much of its effectiveness comes from decision fine-tuning
and how much from the underlying language model. Calibration does not settle the question either: any monotone
transformation of the probabilities leaves a ranking unchanged. We therefore compare decision fine-tuning with plain
reranker fine-tuning of the same backbone, and we test whether the advertised calibration holds for relevance and is
of any use:

Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows how a decision model reranks and what we compare. For six Qwen backbones from 0.6B to
27B parameters, we evaluate the base model asked zero-shot, its decision fine-tunes (Kev and Clef) and its reranker
fine-tunes, including two we train ourselves on MS~MARCO alone (at 9B as a control, at 27B where none has been
released). All of them rerank the same BM25 candidates of TREC DL 2019--2023 and five BEIR collections, and all
return a probability of relevance per passage. Decision and base models are asked whether the passage answers the
query; an ablation asks S1Rank's looser question instead~\cite{S1Rank}. We find:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Reranker fine-tuning beats decision fine-tuning.} From 0.6B to 9B, the reranker fine-tune ranks better
  on every backbone, by 5 to 29 points nDCG@10, significantly on 48 of 60 pairs of backbone and collection, never
  significantly worse, and also on collections that no fine-tune was trained on. At 27B, the two tie. On no backbone
  does decision fine-tuning make the better reranker.
  \item \emph{Up to 9B, decision fine-tuning adds nothing over the base model.} It stays within $-2.5$ and $+0.7$
  points of simply asking the untuned model the same question, while reranker fine-tuning adds 6 to 30 points.
  \item \emph{The calibration advantage comes from the question, not from the model.} Asked whether a passage answers
  the query, decision models are well calibrated. Asked S1Rank's question, the same models lose their advantage over
  reranker fine-tunes while their ranking hardly moves, and the untuned base models gain as much from the wording.
  Up to 9B, calibration does not even buy better passage selection: a reranker fine-tune keeps the better set at a
  fixed threshold.
\end{enumerate}
